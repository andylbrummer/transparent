# down-through-the-earth

> Script for the guide "Down through the Earth". One sentence a line; `caption || spoken` where they differ.
> Figures: Earth radius 6,371 km and the shell depths are PREM (Dziewonski & Anderson 1981), as the app's shells and
> depth ladder use them (app/public/geo/depth-ladder.json). Pressures and densities are PREM; temperatures are the
> ladder's (Anderson 1982). Kola borehole depth: app/public/geo/landmarks.json (12,262 m, reached 1989).

## 01-look-down
Point your phone at the ground, or look straight down.
The ground has gone clear.
What you see is the far side of the planet, 12,742 km away, through the middle of the Earth. || What you see is the far side of the planet, twelve thousand, seven hundred and forty two kilometres away, through the middle of the Earth.

## 02-deepest-hole
Nobody has been more than a few kilometres down.
The deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola borehole in the Russian Arctic, reached 12.3 km in 1989. || The deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola borehole in the Russian Arctic, reached twelve point three kilometres, in nineteen eighty nine.
That is about a five-hundredth of the way to the centre. || That is about a five hundredth of the way to the centre.
Everything below it we know from earthquakes: their waves cross the planet, and the time they take tells us what they passed through.

## 03-crust
First, the crust.
It is about 7 km thick under the oceans, and 30 to 50 km under the continents. || It is about seven kilometres thick under the oceans, and thirty to fifty kilometres under the continents.
For the size of the Earth, that is thinner than an eggshell.
Its base is the Moho, named after Andrija Mohorovičić, who found it in 1909 in the waves of an earthquake in Croatia. || Its base is the Moho, named after Andrija Mohorovichich, who found it in nineteen oh nine, in the waves of an earthquake in Croatia.

## 04-mantle
Below the crust is the mantle, most of the Earth's volume.
It is solid rock, yet it creeps, a few centimetres a year, and carries the plates with it.
At 670 km down, the pressure squeezes its minerals into denser forms. || At six hundred and seventy kilometres down, the pressure squeezes its minerals into denser forms.
That is the top of the lower mantle.

## 05-deep-mantle
At the bottom of the mantle sit two vast regions where earthquake waves slow down.
One lies under Africa, and is nicknamed Tuzo.
The other, Jason, lies under the Pacific.
Each is wider than a continent, and what they are made of is still argued over.

## 06-outer-core
At 2,890 km down, the rock ends. || At two thousand, eight hundred and ninety kilometres down, the rock ends.
Below it is the outer core: liquid iron and nickel, almost twice as dense as the rock above.
Its slow churning makes the Earth's magnetic field.
Shear waves from earthquakes cannot cross a liquid, and that is how we know it is molten.

## 07-inner-core
In 1936, Inge Lehmann noticed faint waves arriving where none should, and worked out that the core has a solid heart. || In nineteen thirty six, Inge Lehmann noticed faint waves arriving where none should, and worked out that the core has a solid heart.
The inner core begins 5,150 km down. || The inner core begins five thousand, one hundred and fifty kilometres down.
It is iron at more than 4,000 °C, kept solid by the pressure. || It is iron at more than four thousand degrees Celsius, kept solid by the pressure.
At the centre, 6,371 km down, the pressure is 3.6 million times that of the air around you. || At the centre, six thousand, three hundred and seventy one kilometres down, the pressure is three point six million times that of the air around you.
Waves that cross the inner core from pole to pole arrive a few seconds early.
Its iron has a grain, and nobody yet knows why.
